# McKinsey 7-S Framework Reference

## Purpose

This reference is the authoritative source for the McKinsey 7-S framework (Strategy,
Structure, Systems, Shared Values, Skills, Style, Staff) — the **static
organizational-alignment / diagnostic lens** of the change-management methodology suite —
consumed by the change-management skill as a **selectable methodology** (routed through
`methodology-selection.md`, the cross-methodology selector) and by the sibling references.
It owns the platform treatment of the seven elements (the hard/soft split and the
Shared-Values center), the **alignment-diagnostic method** — the 21-pair inter-element
consistency assessment with the Shared-Values pairs weighted central, which is 7-S's
distinctive apparatus and its analogue of ADKAR's barrier point — the per-element and
per-pair alignment readout, the cross-methodology selection boundary (7-S vs ADKAR /
Kotter / Lewin / Bridges), and the unified application procedure an agent runs to diagnose
a real organization's alignment.

7-S's lens is the **organization's internal alignment at a point in time**, not the
trajectory of a change through time. It answers a fundamentally different question from the
process methodologies: not "what state is this change in?" (Lewin) or "is each group ready
to adopt?" (ADKAR) or "how do we lead the org through this transformation?" (Kotter) or
"how is each person experiencing the transition?" (Bridges), but **"are the seven elements
of the organization mutually consistent — pulling in the same direction — or are some
drifting out of alignment with the others?"** This is a **static snapshot**, not a
sequence: 7-S has no stages, no steps, no zones, and no temporal gate. It is the
**alignment check applied before, during, or after any of the process methodologies'
stages** — the diagnostic that tells you *whether the organization's parts cohere*, which
the temporal methodologies then move. The sibling methodologies already place 7-S this way
in their cross-methodology tables ("the org's seven elements · static snapshot (no
sequence) · org elements drift out of alignment · diagnose alignment at any stage"); this
document owns the canonical treatment underneath that placement. The cross-methodology
selection boundary lives in §6 and feeds the selector.

## 1. The 7-S Model (orientation) — the static alignment frame

The 7-S model treats an organization as **seven interdependent elements that must be
mutually aligned** for the organization to be effective. Its founding claim — the reason it
is drawn as a web rather than a list or a sequence — is that **the seven elements are
interconnected: a change in any one ripples to all the others, and the organization
performs well only when all seven are coherent and reinforce one another**. There is no
"first" element and no "last" element; there is no flow. 7-S diagnoses a *configuration*,
asking of every element-pair whether the two are consistent. The seven group into three
**hard** elements (tangible, easier to identify and change directly) and four **soft**
elements (intangible, culture-driven, harder to change but the deeper determinant of
success), with **Shared Values at the center**, connected to all six others.

| Element | Group | What it is (1 line) | The alignment question it raises |
|---------|-------|---------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Strategy** | Hard | The plan to build and sustain competitive advantage — how the org intends to win | Does the strategy fit the structure, systems, and values — or does it assume an org the other elements aren't? |
| **Structure** | Hard | How the org is arranged — reporting lines, hierarchy, division of work, who answers to whom | Does the structure enable the strategy and reflect the values, or does it fight them? |
| **Systems** | Hard | The daily procedures, processes, and workflows — how work actually gets done and is measured | Do the systems (incl. incentives and metrics) reinforce the strategy and the desired behaviors, or reward the opposite? |
| **Shared Values** | Soft (**center**) | The core beliefs, norms, and culture at the heart of the org — what it actually stands for | Do all six other elements express the shared values, or do some contradict what the org claims to value? |
| **Skills** | Soft | The actual competencies and capabilities of the organization and its people | Do the org's real capabilities match what the strategy and systems demand? |
| **Style** | Soft | The leadership and management style — how leaders actually behave, what they pay attention to | Does how leaders behave reinforce the strategy and embody the values, or undercut them? |
| **Staff** | Soft | The people — the workforce, its composition, capabilities, recruiting and development | Are the right people, in the right numbers, with the right development, present to run the strategy? |

**Interconnection principle (the model's whole claim):** The seven elements are a **web,
not a sequence** — every element is connected to every other, and **Shared Values is
connected to all six**. The organization is effective only when all seven are **aligned**
(mutually consistent and reinforcing); a single misaligned element drags on every element
it contradicts. This is why 7-S is a *diagnostic of consistency across pairs* (§4) rather
than a checklist of elements in isolation: an element can be individually excellent and
still be a problem if it is out of alignment with the others (a brilliant strategy the
structure cannot execute; a stated value the incentive system contradicts).

### Why hard vs soft — and why Shared Values is the center

| Distinction | What it means | Why it matters for diagnosis |
|-------------|---------------|------------------------------|
| **Hard elements** (Strategy, Structure, Systems) | Tangible, documented, directly controllable by management; visible on org charts, strategy decks, and process maps | Easy to *identify* and change by decree — and therefore the elements managers over-rely on, mistaking a re-org or a new strategy deck for real change |
| **Soft elements** (Shared Values, Skills, Style, Staff) | Intangible, culture- and people-driven; not on any chart; changed slowly and indirectly | Harder to see and change, but the **deeper determinant of whether change holds**; a hard-element change that ignores the soft elements is fought by the culture it never touched |
| **Shared Values at the center** | The core ideology the org actually lives by; drawn at the hub, connected to all six others | Shared Values is the element the other six must each cohere *with*; a misalignment between Shared Values and any other element is the **most load-bearing kind of misalignment**, because the central element touches everything. This is why the §4 diagnostic weights the Shared-Values pairs central. |

## 2. The Hard Elements — Strategy, Structure, Systems

**What they are:** The three tangible, directly-manageable elements. They are the ones a
leadership team can change by decision — rewrite the strategy, redraw the org chart, deploy
a new system — which is exactly why they are the elements change efforts tend to start and
stop with. The 7-S discipline is to treat a hard-element change as *one element of seven*,
and immediately ask what it now contradicts among the other six.

| Hard element | Observable "what it is" | Misalignment signal (out of sync with the rest) | What good alignment looks like |
|--------------|-------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| **Strategy** | The articulated plan for competitive advantage and the choices that follow from it | The strategy presumes capabilities (Skills), people (Staff), or a structure the org does not have; or it contradicts what the org says it values | Strategy is achievable with the org's actual Skills/Staff, executable by its Structure, measured by its Systems, and expresses its Shared Values |
| **Structure** | The org chart — reporting lines, grouping of work, centralization vs decentralization | Structure routes work in ways the strategy fights (e.g., functional silos under a strategy that needs cross-functional speed); structure contradicts the stated values (e.g., rigid hierarchy under a "we empower people" value) | Structure channels work the way the strategy needs and embodies the values; decision rights sit where the strategy needs them |
| **Systems** | The processes, procedures, workflows, IT systems, **incentives, and metrics** that run the daily work | Systems reward the behavior the strategy and values *oppose* (the classic "we value teamwork but pay on individual numbers" contradiction); processes enforce the old way | Systems — especially incentives and metrics — reinforce the strategy and reward the behaviors the shared values call for |

**Failure mode (hard-element):** *Mistaking a hard-element change for organizational
change.* Re-drawing the structure or publishing a new strategy is highly visible and feels
like progress, but if the soft elements (Skills, Style, Staff, Shared Values) and the other
hard elements are not brought into alignment with it, the change is fought by everything it
contradicts. A new strategy the systems still penalize, executed by a structure that routes
work the old way, staffed by people without the skills it assumes, is a strategy deck — not
a changed organization.

**Composes with:** Kotter's **Step 5 (Empower broad-based action)** is the org-level
realignment of "hostile structures and systems" — the *process* of bringing Structure and
Systems into line; `kotter-8-step.md` owns that step's sequence. 7-S owns the *diagnosis*
that those hard elements are misaligned and *with what*. Reference `kotter-8-step.md` for
the realignment sequence; do not restate the 8-step process here.

## 3. The Soft Elements — Shared Values, Skills, Style, Staff

**What they are:** The four intangible, culture- and people-driven elements. They are not on
any chart and cannot be changed by decree; they change slowly, through behavior over time.
They are also the **deeper determinant of whether a change holds** — the elements a
hard-element-only change leaves untouched and is then defeated by. **Shared Values is the
center** of this group and of the whole model.

| Soft element | Observable "what it is" | Misalignment signal (out of sync with the rest) | What good alignment looks like |
|--------------|-------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| **Shared Values** (center) | The core beliefs and norms the org actually lives by (distinct from the values on the poster) | The lived values contradict the strategy, the incentives, or how leaders behave; the *stated* values and the *enacted* values diverge | All six other elements visibly express the same core values; what the org says it stands for is what its structure, systems, and leaders actually do |
| **Skills** | The real, demonstrated competencies and capabilities of the org and its people | The strategy and systems demand capabilities the org does not have; a capability gap sits unaddressed under an ambitious strategy | The org's actual capabilities match what the strategy, systems, and structure require; capability gaps are named and closed |
| **Style** | How leaders actually behave and what they attend to (not their titles) | Leaders' behavior contradicts the strategy or the stated values (the "do as I say" gap); attention goes to what the strategy says is unimportant | Leadership behavior reinforces the strategy and embodies the shared values; leaders attend to what the strategy says matters |
| **Staff** | The people — workforce composition, numbers, recruiting, and development | The people in place (numbers, mix, development) cannot run the strategy; recruiting/development is misaligned with the capabilities the strategy needs | The right people, in the right numbers, developed in the directions the strategy requires, are present and growing |

**Failure mode (soft-element):** *Changing the hard elements and assuming the soft ones
follow.* The soft elements are invisible and slow, so they are the ones a change effort
neglects — and they are precisely where a hard-element change is defeated. A new structure
under an unchanged leadership Style, a new strategy without the Skills to run it, a new
system against unchanged Shared Values: in each case the soft element the change ignored
pulls it back. Soft elements are harder to change *and* the deeper determinant of success —
the worst combination to neglect.

**Composes with:** `adkar-framework.md` owns the *individual-readiness* layer (per-person
Awareness→Reinforcement scoring) and `bridges-transition.md` the *psychological-transition*
layer — both of which operate on the people the 7-S **Staff/Skills/Style** elements
describe at the org level. 7-S diagnoses whether those elements are *aligned with the rest
of the organization at a point in time*; ADKAR and Bridges then move the individuals
through the change. Reference those siblings for the per-person layers; do not restate them
here.

## 4. The Alignment-Diagnostic Method — CANONICAL (the 21-pair consistency assessment)

This is 7-S's distinctive apparatus and the section a 7-S diagnosis reads from. Where ADKAR
computes a **barrier point**, Kotter an **earliest-weak-step**, Lewin a **net force**, and
Bridges an **earliest-weak-zone**, 7-S computes **pairwise alignment** — it scores the
**consistency of every pair of elements** and identifies the **weakest-aligned pair(s)** to
fix. Because the model's whole claim is interconnection (a change in one element ripples to
all), the diagnostic is inherently *pairwise*: an element is never assessed in isolation,
only against each of the others.

Seven elements yield **C(7,2) = 21 unique element-pairs**. The diagnostic scores each pair
for how consistent the two elements are with each other, then derives where the
organization is out of alignment.

### The 21 element-pairs

| # | Pair | # | Pair | # | Pair |
|---|------|---|------|---|------|
| 1 | Strategy ↔ Structure | 8 | Structure ↔ Skills | 15 | **Shared Values ↔ Strategy** |
| 2 | Strategy ↔ Systems | 9 | Structure ↔ Style | 16 | **Shared Values ↔ Structure** |
| 3 | Strategy ↔ Skills | 10 | Structure ↔ Staff | 17 | **Shared Values ↔ Systems** |
| 4 | Strategy ↔ Style | 11 | Systems ↔ Skills | 18 | **Shared Values ↔ Skills** |
| 5 | Strategy ↔ Staff | 12 | Systems ↔ Style | 19 | **Shared Values ↔ Style** |
| 6 | Structure ↔ Systems | 13 | Systems ↔ Staff | 20 | **Shared Values ↔ Staff** |
| 7 | Skills ↔ Style | 14 | Skills ↔ Staff | 21 | Style ↔ Staff |

Pairs 15-20 (bold) are the **six Shared-Values pairs** — Shared Values against each of the
other six elements. These are weighted **central** (see the weighting rule below): because
Shared Values is the hub the other six must each cohere with, a misalignment on a
Shared-Values pair is more load-bearing than a misalignment between two peripheral
elements.

### Pairwise Alignment Scale (1-5) — CANONICAL

Score each of the 21 pairs 1-5 for how consistent the two elements are with each other,
grounded in observable evidence of how the elements actually behave (not how they are
described on paper).

| Score | Label | Observable Indicators (level) |
|-------|-------|-------------------------------|
| 5 | **Fully aligned** | The two elements actively reinforce each other; each makes the other stronger; no contradiction |
| 4 | **Aligned** | Broadly consistent; minor friction that does not undermine either element |
| 3 | **Partial** | Consistent in some respects, contradictory in others; real tension present under pressure |
| 2 | **Misaligned** | The two elements work against each other; one materially undermines the other |
| 1 | **Contradictory** | The two elements directly oppose each other; one negates the other (e.g., incentives that punish the stated strategy) |

### Shared-Values Central-Weighting Rule — CANONICAL

The six Shared-Values pairs (15-20) carry **double weight** in the diagnostic, reflecting
the model's structure (Shared Values is connected to all six others at the hub). This is
7-S's distinctive judgment: not all misalignments are equal — a contradiction between an
element and the **central** Shared Values element is the most consequential kind, because
the central element touches everything.

```
weighted_pair_score(pair):
  IF pair involves Shared Values (pairs 15-20):  weight = 2
  ELSE (the 15 peripheral pairs):                weight = 1

A Shared-Values pair scoring <=2 is a CENTRAL misalignment — flag it first regardless of
how many peripheral pairs are weak, because the central element it contradicts propagates
to every other element.
```

### Lowest-Alignment Decision Rule — CANONICAL (the barrier-point analogue)

```
SCORE all 21 element-pairs 1-5 per the scale above (ground each in observable behavior).

  central_misalignments    = Shared-Values pairs (15-20) scoring <= 2
  peripheral_misalignments = the other pairs (1-14, 21) scoring <= 2

  weakest_pair = the pair with the lowest score; on ties, the Shared-Values pair
                 (central weight) is the weakest by the weighting rule.

  alignment_state diagnosis:
    IF any central_misalignment exists (a Shared-Values pair <= 2):
      STATE   = CORE MISALIGNMENT
      ACTION  = realign the contradicting element WITH the Shared Values first — the central
                element propagates to all six; fixing a peripheral pair while the core is
                contradicted is wasted (the culture pulls the change back).
    ELSE IF any peripheral_misalignment exists (a non-SV pair <= 2):
      STATE   = PERIPHERAL MISALIGNMENT
      ACTION  = realign the weakest pair; bring the two contradicting elements into
                consistency before relying on either.
    ELSE IF any pair = 3 (none <= 2):
      STATE   = PARTIAL ALIGNMENT (monitor; tension under pressure)
      ACTION  = tighten the partially-aligned pairs.
    ELSE (all pairs >= 4):
      STATE   = ALIGNED
```

**Realign-the-core-first rule (CANONICAL):** When a Shared-Values pair is misaligned (a
central misalignment), **realign that element with the Shared Values before fixing any
peripheral pair.** Because Shared Values is the hub connected to all six other elements, a
contradiction there propagates outward — an incentive system (Systems) that contradicts the
stated values poisons every behavior the values were meant to drive, no matter how well the
peripheral pairs align. Fixing a Strategy↔Structure misalignment while the Systems↔Shared-Values
pair is contradictory is the 7-S analogue of sending someone to Knowledge training when
their Desire is the barrier (ADKAR §4): you are investing past the real constraint. This is
the single most important judgment in a 7-S diagnosis and the most common place a junior
analyst goes wrong (see Anti-Patterns).

**Ripple rule:** Because the elements are interconnected, **any deliberate change to one
element re-opens the alignment question for every pair that element belongs to.** A 7-S
diagnosis is re-run after a change to confirm the change did not break a pair that was
previously aligned — the way a re-org (Structure) can silently misalign Structure↔Skills
(the new structure needs capabilities the org lacks) or Structure↔Shared-Values (the new
hierarchy contradicts an "empowerment" value).

## 5. Per-Element Alignment Readout (1-5)

In addition to the 21 pairwise scores, summarize each element's **overall alignment** with
the rest of the organization — the aggregate of the pairs that element participates in
(each element appears in 6 of the 21 pairs). This element-level readout mirrors the 1-5
readout the sibling methodologies use for their stages/steps/zones, keeping the suite
consistent, and gives a fast read on *which element is most out of step with the whole*.

| Status | Label | What it means for the element |
|--------|-------|-------------------------------|
| 5 | **Fully aligned** | This element reinforces, and is reinforced by, all six others; no contradictions |
| 4 | **Aligned** | Broadly consistent with the rest; minor friction on one or two pairs |
| 3 | **Partial** | Consistent with some elements, in tension with others; at risk under pressure |
| 2 | **Misaligned** | This element works against several of the others; a drag on the configuration |
| 1 | **Contradictory** | This element directly opposes the rest; the dominant misalignment in the org |

**Element-readout rule:** The element with the lowest alignment readout is the **dominant
misalignment** — the element to bring into line first (if it is **Shared Values**, the
whole configuration is at stake and it is unconditionally first, per the realign-the-core-first
rule). Unlike the sibling methodologies, there is **no sequence** to the readout: the
elements are assessed simultaneously as a static configuration, and "first" means *most
misaligned*, not *earliest in a sequence*.

## 6. When to Use 7-S vs ADKAR / Kotter / Lewin / Bridges

This is the **cross-methodology selection surface** — a feed to the methodology selector
(`methodology-selection.md`, the cluster-canonical home for choosing among the change
methodologies; it reads the differentiators below to recommend a methodology for a given
change). Until that selector is consulted, this table is the 7-S-anchored view of the
boundary. The two selection axes are **unit of change** (what the methodology operates on)
and **dominant risk** (the risk it is best suited to manage). 7-S is the **outlier on the
temporal axis**: it is the only methodology in the suite with **no temporal shape** — a
static snapshot rather than a sequence/state/phase — and the only one whose job is
*diagnosis of alignment* rather than *driving a change through time*.

| Methodology | Unit of change | Temporal shape | Dominant risk it manages | Best when | Relationship to the process methodologies |
|-------------|----------------|----------------|--------------------------|-----------|--------------------------------------------|
| **7-S (this doc)** | The org's seven elements | **Static snapshot (no sequence)** | Org elements drift out of alignment with each other | You need to **diagnose alignment** — whether Strategy/Structure/Systems/Shared-Values/Skills/Style/Staff cohere — before, during, or after a change | The **alignment check** run at any point along any of the others' stages |
| **Lewin (3-Stage)** | The change state | State model (unfreeze → change → refreeze) | Over-complicating a simple change; skipping refreeze | You need the simplest change-state frame, or the foundational map situating the others | The temporal meta-frame; 7-S diagnoses alignment at any of its stages |
| **Kotter (8-Step)** | The organization (top-down) | Sequence (8 steps) | Large transformation stalls / loses momentum | Org-wide, urgency-driven transformation you need to *lead the org through* | 7-S diagnoses the alignment Kotter Step 5 then realigns |
| **ADKAR** | The individual (per person/group) | Sequence (5 elements) | A group is not individually ready to adopt | You need to know *whether each group is ready* and where each is blocked | Operates on the people the 7-S Staff/Skills elements describe at the org level |
| **Bridges (Transition)** | The person's psychology | Phase model (Ending → Neutral Zone → New Beginning) | Emotional/identity disruption is mishandled | Identity/emotional disruption is the dominant risk under any structural change | The human-experience layer under the change 7-S diagnoses structurally |

**7-S is the diagnostic, the others are the process (they are complementary, not
competing):** 7-S does not move a change through time — it takes a *picture* of the
organization and tells you whether the seven parts cohere. The process methodologies then
*act* on what the picture reveals: Lewin frames the change-state arc, Kotter leads the org
through it, ADKAR confirms each group is ready, Bridges handles the emotional passage. The
canonical composition is to **run 7-S first as the alignment diagnosis** (what is out of
sync, and is the core involved?), **then choose the process methodology** the realignment
needs (Kotter to lead an org-wide realignment; ADKAR for per-group readiness; Bridges for
the emotional cost; Lewin as the frame) — and to **re-run 7-S after** to confirm the change
did not break a previously-aligned pair (the ripple rule, §4). 7-S is also the natural lens
when the presenting problem is *not* a planned change at all but a felt incoherence
("strategy says one thing, the incentives reward another") — it diagnoses the misalignment
that a change then fixes.

**Selection summary:** choose **7-S** when the need is an **alignment diagnostic** — when
you need to know *whether the organization's seven elements cohere* (before launching a
change, to find what is misaligned; during, to check the change has not broken alignment;
after, to confirm the new configuration holds), or when a felt incoherence rather than a
planned change is the presenting problem; choose **Lewin** for the simplest change-state
frame; **Kotter** for a whole-org transformation at risk of stalling; **ADKAR** when the
unit is the individual/group and the risk is non-adoption; **Bridges** when emotional
transition is the risk. 7-S **composes** with all of them — it is the diagnosis run before,
during, and after the process the others drive (see `methodology-selection.md` for the
combine-methodologies guidance).

## 7. Unified Application Procedure

This is the deterministic procedure an agent executes to apply 7-S to a real organization.
It ties §§1-5 into a runnable flow. Note it is a **diagnostic** procedure, not a
sequence-execution procedure: it produces an alignment picture, not a staged plan.

```
1. CONFIRM 7-S is the right methodology per §6:
   - the need is an ALIGNMENT DIAGNOSTIC (do the seven elements cohere?), OR a felt
     incoherence is the presenting problem (not a staged change to lead).
   (If you need to lead the org through a transformation, route to Kotter; if you need
    per-group readiness, route to ADKAR; if emotional transition is the risk, route to
    Bridges; if the simplest change-state frame, route to Lewin. 7-S composes with all —
    run it as the diagnosis, then pick the process. See §6 / methodology-selection.md.)

2. CHARACTERIZE the seven elements as they ACTUALLY are (observable behavior, not the
   paper description): Strategy, Structure, Systems, Shared Values, Skills, Style, Staff.

3. SCORE all 21 element-pairs 1-5 per the §4 scale, grounded in observable evidence.
   Apply the Shared-Values central weight (pairs 15-20, weight 2; §4).

4. COMPUTE per §4:
   - central_misalignments    = Shared-Values pairs (15-20) scoring <= 2
   - peripheral_misalignments = other pairs scoring <= 2
   - weakest_pair             = lowest-scored pair (ties -> the Shared-Values pair)
   - alignment_state          = CORE MISALIGNMENT / PERIPHERAL MISALIGNMENT /
                                PARTIAL ALIGNMENT / ALIGNED
   Also COMPUTE the per-element alignment readout (§5); identify the dominant-misalignment
   element (lowest readout; Shared Values is unconditionally first if it is <= 2).

5. EMIT:
   - the seven-element characterization (what each element actually is),
   - the 21-pair alignment scores (with the Shared-Values pairs marked central),
   - the per-element alignment readout (7 rows, 1-5),
   - the alignment_state + the weakest pair and the dominant-misalignment element,
   - the realignment focus: which element to bring into line FIRST (realign the core
     first if a Shared-Values pair is <=2, per §4), and with which element(s) it must be
     made consistent,
   - the ripple note: which previously-aligned pairs to re-check after the realignment.
```

An agent that can execute steps 1-5 from this document alone produces a seven-element
characterization, 21 pairwise alignment scores, the per-element readout, the identified
weakest pair and dominant misalignment, and the realignment focus — the outputs a 7-S
alignment diagnosis requires.

## 8. Worked Example — SaaS Company Shifting to Product-Led Growth

A fully-worked application of the model to one named situation: a **mid-size SaaS company
shifting its strategy from sales-led (enterprise field sales) to product-led growth (PLG —
self-serve, bottom-up adoption)**. The presenting problem is a felt incoherence — "we say
we're product-led now, but nothing else has moved" — which is exactly the static-alignment
diagnosis 7-S is for (§6). This demonstrates the §7 procedure and serves as the regression
fixture.

### Step 2: Seven-Element Characterization (as they actually are)

| Element | What it actually is (observed) |
|---------|--------------------------------|
| **Strategy** | Newly declared product-led growth: win via self-serve adoption and bottom-up expansion |
| **Structure** | Unchanged enterprise sales org — large field-sales team, sales-region hierarchy, no growth/PLG function |
| **Systems** | Compensation still pays on closed enterprise deals; metrics still track ARR-per-rep, not activation/self-serve conversion |
| **Shared Values** | Lived value is "the rep owns the customer relationship"; sales heroism is celebrated |
| **Skills** | Deep enterprise-selling capability; little product-analytics, growth-engineering, or self-serve-onboarding capability |
| **Style** | Leadership attention and praise go to big closed deals; QBRs are sales-pipeline reviews |
| **Staff** | Predominantly field-sales hires; few growth PMs, growth engineers, or lifecycle-marketing staff |

### Step 3: 21-Pair Alignment Scores (Shared-Values pairs marked **central**)

| # | Pair | Score | # | Pair | Score |
|---|------|-------|---|------|-------|
| 1 | Strategy ↔ Structure | 1 — Contradictory (PLG strategy, pure field-sales structure) | 12 | Systems ↔ Style | 4 — Aligned (both reward deals) |
| 2 | Strategy ↔ Systems | 1 — Contradictory (PLG strategy, deal-based comp/metrics) | 13 | Systems ↔ Staff | 4 — Aligned (sales comp fits sales staff) |
| 3 | Strategy ↔ Skills | 2 — Misaligned (PLG needs growth/analytics skills absent) | 14 | Skills ↔ Staff | 5 — Fully aligned (sales skills, sales staff) |
| 4 | Strategy ↔ Style | 2 — Misaligned (leaders still attend to big deals) | 15 | **Shared Values ↔ Strategy** | **1 — Contradictory** ("rep owns the customer" vs self-serve) |
| 5 | Strategy ↔ Staff | 2 — Misaligned (no growth staff for PLG) | 16 | **Shared Values ↔ Structure** | **4 — Aligned** (sales-ownership value fits sales structure) |
| 6 | Structure ↔ Systems | 5 — Fully aligned (sales structure, sales comp) | 17 | **Shared Values ↔ Systems** | **4 — Aligned** (rep-ownership value fits deal comp) |
| 7 | Skills ↔ Style | 4 — Aligned (sales skills, sales-focused leaders) | 18 | **Shared Values ↔ Skills** | **3 — Partial** |
| 8 | Structure ↔ Skills | 4 — Aligned (sales structure, sales skills) | 19 | **Shared Values ↔ Style** | **5 — Fully aligned** (both celebrate rep heroism) |
| 9 | Structure ↔ Style | 4 — Aligned | 20 | **Shared Values ↔ Staff** | **4 — Aligned** (sales-ownership value fits sales staff) |
| 10 | Structure ↔ Staff | 5 — Fully aligned (sales structure, sales staff) | 21 | Style ↔ Staff | 4 — Aligned |
| 11 | Systems ↔ Skills | 4 — Aligned | | | |

### Step 4: Compute (per §4-§5)

- **central_misalignments (Shared-Values pairs <=2):** pair 15, **Shared Values ↔ Strategy = 1**
  (Contradictory). The lived value "the rep owns the customer relationship" directly opposes
  the new self-serve PLG strategy. This is a **core misalignment**.
- **peripheral_misalignments (<=2):** pairs 1 (Strategy↔Structure = 1), 2 (Strategy↔Systems
  = 1), 3, 4, 5 — all the Strategy pairs against the operating elements.
- **weakest_pair:** three pairs tie at 1 — Strategy↔Structure, Strategy↔Systems, and the
  central Shared-Values↔Strategy. Per the tie rule, the **central pair (Shared Values ↔
  Strategy)** is the weakest by the weighting rule.
- **alignment_state:** a Shared-Values pair is <=2 → **CORE MISALIGNMENT.**
- **Per-element readout (§5):** **Strategy = 1 — Contradictory** (it opposes Structure,
  Systems, Shared Values, Skills, Style, Staff — all six of its pairs are <=2; it is the dominant
  misalignment element). Shared Values = 3 — Partial (aligned with the *old* world, but
  contradictory with the new Strategy). Structure, Systems, Skills, Style, Staff are each
  internally aligned with one another — they cohere as a *sales-led* organization; only the

- **21-pair scores + per-element readout:** produced above (Strategy = 1, the dominant

- **Alignment state:** **CORE MISALIGNMENT** — the new Strategy contradicts the central

- **Realignment focus (realign-the-core-first, §4):** realign **Shared Values** with the new

## Anti-Patterns

| Anti-Pattern | Signal | Root Cause | Remediation |
|-------------|--------|-----------|-------------|
| **Treating 7-S as a temporal sequence** | The seven S's are worked "in order" (Strategy first, Staff last) as if they were stages; an element is "done" and the diagnosis moves on | Importing the sequence-gate mental model from Kotter/ADKAR/Lewin/Bridges onto a model that has no sequence | 7-S is a **static snapshot** (§1, §6) — assess all seven *simultaneously* as a configuration and score the 21 *pairs*; "first" means *most misaligned* (§5), never *earliest in a sequence* |
| **Changing only the hard elements** | A re-org or a new strategy deck is published and the change is declared made; Skills/Style/Staff/Shared-Values are never touched | Hard elements are visible and directly controllable, so they feel like "the change"; soft elements are invisible and slow | Diagnose all 21 pairs (§4); a hard-element change that leaves the soft elements misaligned with it will be defeated by them — realign the soft elements it now contradicts, especially Shared Values |
| **Ignoring the central Shared-Values misalignment** | A peripheral pair (e.g., Strategy↔Structure) is fixed while a Shared-Values pair stays contradictory; the change keeps failing for unexplained reasons | Treating all 21 pairs as equal weight; missing that the central element propagates to all six | Apply the central-weighting and realign-the-core-first rules (§4) — a Shared-Values pair <=2 is fixed *before* any peripheral pair, because the central element contradicts everything downstream |
| **Scoring the paper, not the practice** | Alignment scores reflect the strategy deck, the org chart, and the values poster — the *stated* elements — rather than how the org actually behaves | Reluctance to name the gap between the espoused and the enacted (the stated value vs the lived value) | Ground every pair score in observable behavior (§4) — score the *lived* Shared Values against the *actual* Systems (what incentives really reward), not the poster against the policy |
| **Diagnosing once and never re-checking after a change** | A 7-S diagnosis is run before the change; the change is made; alignment is assumed to hold and never re-assessed | Forgetting the interconnection/ripple principle — that changing one element re-opens the alignment question for all its pairs | Re-run the diagnosis after the change (the ripple rule, §4); confirm the realignment did not silently break a previously-aligned pair (a re-org that newly misaligns Structure↔Skills) |

## Behavioral Markers

| Dimension | Principal Behavior | Junior Behavior |
|-----------|-------------------|----------------|
| **Static-vs-temporal discipline** | Treats 7-S as a static alignment snapshot — assesses all seven elements at once and scores the 21 pairs; never imposes a sequence | Works the S's "in order" as if they were stages; mistakes the diagnostic for a staged plan |
| **Pairwise rigor** | Scores every element-*pair* for consistency, grounded in observable behavior; never assesses an element in isolation | Rates each element on its own merits; misses that an excellent element can be a problem if misaligned with the others |
| **Central-Shared-Values discipline** | Applies the central weight; fixes a Shared-Values misalignment before any peripheral pair; explains why the hub propagates | Treats all pairs as equal; fixes the visible Strategy↔Structure pair while the core value contradiction poisons everything |
| **Espoused-vs-enacted honesty** | Scores the *lived* values and *actual* incentives, naming the gap between the poster and the practice | Scores the strategy deck and the values poster; rates a "we value teamwork" org as aligned while it pays on individual numbers |
| **Hard/soft balance** | Diagnoses the soft elements (Skills/Style/Staff/Shared-Values) as rigorously as the hard ones; realigns what a hard-element change contradicts | Changes the structure and the strategy and stops; assumes the soft elements will follow a re-org |
| **Ripple awareness** | Re-runs the diagnosis after a change to catch newly-broken pairs; treats every element change as re-opening its pairs | Diagnoses once, makes the change, and assumes alignment holds; never re-checks |
