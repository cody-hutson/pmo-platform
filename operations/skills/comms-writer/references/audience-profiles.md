# Audience Profiles — PMO Communications

## Purpose

This file defines the stakeholder classification frameworks, commitment tracking
models, and resistance diagnosis tools that the comms-writer skill uses to
calibrate communications to their target audience. Read this file when calibrating
any communication to a specific audience or when designing engagement strategies.

---

## 1. Mendelow's Power/Interest Grid

Stakeholders classify into four quadrants based on their power to influence the
initiative and their interest in it. Classification must be honest — not aspirational.

```
                    HIGH INTEREST
                         |
    Keep Informed        |        Manage Closely
    (Low Power,          |        (High Power,
     High Interest)      |         High Interest)
                         |
   ──────────────────────┼──────────────────────
                         |
    Monitor              |        Keep Satisfied
    (Low Power,          |        (High Power,
     Low Interest)       |         Low Interest)
                         |
                    LOW INTEREST
```

### Quadrant Engagement Strategies

| Quadrant | Power | Interest | Strategy | Communication Frequency | Communication Depth | Risk If Mismanaged |
|----------|-------|----------|----------|------------------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Manage Closely** | High | High | Regular engagement; involve in decisions; highest communication priority; active feedback loops | Weekly or per-event | Full detail + decision framing | Blockers, scope creep, political escalation |
| **Keep Satisfied** | High | Low | Periodic updates; do not overwhelm with detail; ensure no surprises; pre-brief before public announcements | Monthly or at milestones | Executive summary; headlines only | Surprise reaction, late-stage veto, withdrawal of support |
| **Keep Informed** | Low | High | Regular communication; leverage as advocates; potential future power shift; feed their interest | Biweekly or per-milestone | Moderate detail; progress + impact | Loss of advocate base; unaddressed concerns spreading informally |
| **Monitor** | Low | Low | Minimal effort; periodic check for position changes; automated updates acceptable | Quarterly or on major events | Minimal; awareness-level only | Position shift goes undetected |

### Classification Rules

1. **Honesty over aspiration.** Place stakeholders where they ARE, not where you want them. The most common classification error is aspirational placement.
2. **Monthly review rhythm.** Positions shift at milestones, scope changes, and reorgs. Set a minimum monthly review cadence for the stakeholder map.
3. **Identify informal influencers.** Map beyond the org chart. Ask: "Whose opinion changes others' minds?" Track who talks to whom before and after meetings.
4. **Watch for power shifts.** A "Keep Informed" stakeholder who gains a new executive role becomes "Manage Closely" overnight. Detect transitions proactively.
5. **Never assume stability.** A stakeholder's quadrant at project start may not be their quadrant at go-live. Treat the map as a living artifact.

## 2. Commitment Curve Model

Stakeholder commitment progresses through five stages. Each stage requires
different interventions to advance to the next. Regression is possible at any stage.

| Stage | State | Behavioral Markers | Intervention to Advance | Communication Approach |
|-------|-------|--------------------|------------------------|----------------------|
| **1. Unaware** | Don't know the change is coming | No mention of the initiative; asks "what project?" when referenced | Initial communications, announcements, awareness campaigns | Broad, simple, high-level: "What is happening and when" |
| **2. Aware** | Know about it but don't understand implications | Acknowledges the project exists; cannot articulate personal impact | Detailed briefings, Q&A sessions, impact assessments, 1-on-1 conversations | Targeted: "What this means for you specifically" |
| **3. Understanding** | Comprehend what changes and why | Can explain the change to others; asks specific questions about implementation | Involvement in design, participation in decisions, addressing specific concerns, pilot programs | Participatory: "Your input shapes this" |
| **4. Committed** | Actively support; willing to invest effort | Volunteers for activities; defends the initiative when challenged; allocates own resources | Recognition, formal roles (champion, super-user), peer influence platforms | Empowering: "You are part of making this work" |
| **5. Advocating** | Champion the change; influence others | Proactively promotes the initiative; coaches peers; provides unsolicited positive feedback | Champion network leadership, speaking opportunities, success story platforms | Amplifying: "Your voice carries this forward" |

### Minimum Commitment Level

Not all stakeholders need to reach Advocating. The target stage depends on the
stakeholder's role and Power/Interest position.

| Stakeholder Type | Minimum Target Stage | Rationale |
|-----------------|---------------------|-----------|
| Executive sponsor | Advocating | Must actively champion; passive sponsorship (SINO) is an anti-pattern |
| Steering committee member | Committed | Must invest effort in governance decisions; Understanding alone is insufficient |
| Project team member | Committed | Must actively participate in delivery; passive compliance produces poor outcomes |
| End user (direct impact) | Understanding | Must comprehend what changes and be able to follow new processes |
| End user (indirect impact) | Aware | Must know the change exists to avoid surprise; deeper engagement not required |
| Vendor / Consultant | Committed | Must invest effort to deliver; contractual commitment alone is insufficient |

### Regression Triggers

Commitment can regress due to:
- **Broken promises** — announced dates missed without explanation (Understanding → Aware)
- **Negative experiences** — system failures, poor training, unresolved issues (Committed → Understanding)
- **Conflicting priorities** — competing initiatives absorb attention (Committed → Aware)
- **Leadership withdrawal** — sponsor disengages (Advocating → Committed or lower across the board)
- **Scope creep without consultation** — changes imposed without involvement (Understanding → Aware)

When regression is detected, diagnose the cause before selecting an intervention.
Repeating the original stage intervention often fails because the stakeholder has
additional grievance context that the initial intervention did not address.

## 3. Resistance Model — Maurer's Three Levels

Rick Maurer's Three Levels of Resistance require fundamentally different
interventions. Applying Level 1 solutions to Level 3 resistance makes it worse.

| Level | Expression | Root Cause | Required Intervention | Communication Strategy | Diagnostic Questions |
|-------|-----------|------------|----------------------|----------------------|---------------------|
| **Level 1: "I Don't Get It"** | Questions, confusion, requests for more information, misunderstanding scope | Information gap — the person lacks facts or context | Education, data, demonstrations, detailed briefings, Q&A sessions | Informational: facts, timelines, impact summaries, FAQs | "What specific questions do you have?" "What part is unclear?" |
| **Level 2: "I Don't Like It"** | Emotional pushback, anxiety, anger, grief over lost status/comfort, "this is a bad idea" | Perceived loss — the person understands but feels threatened | Empathy, involvement in design, addressing fears directly, acknowledging loss, transition support | Empathetic + participatory: "We hear your concern; here's how we're addressing it" | "What concerns you most?" "What would you lose?" |
| **Level 3: "I Don't Trust You"** | Skepticism, withdrawal, "we've seen this before," refusal to engage, arms-crossed body language | Relationship/credibility deficit — the person doesn't trust the people or organization driving the change | Trust-building, consistent follow-through on small commitments, leadership presence, transparent decision-making, time | Trust-building: actions over words; small wins; visible leadership commitment | "What would it take for you to believe this is different?" "What happened last time?" |

### Resistance Escalation Types

Resistance manifests in escalating forms. Later forms are harder to detect and
more damaging:

| Type | Visibility | Detection Method | Damage Potential |
|------|-----------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Overt challenging** | High (visible) | Direct confrontation in meetings, written objections | Low — easiest to address because it's on the surface |
| **Passive agreement without action** | Medium (semi-visible) | Agrees in meetings but misses deadlines, "forgets" to implement | Medium — insidious because it looks like compliance |
| **Compliance without commitment** | Low (hidden) | Follows minimum letter of process but undermines spirit; malicious compliance | High — produces technical adoption without behavioral change |
| **Deliberate sabotage** | Very low (covert) | Spreading misinformation, actively recruiting opposition, undermining training | Very high — requires leadership intervention and may require HR involvement |

### Kotter & Schlesinger's Six Response Strategies

An escalating spectrum from collaborative to coercive. Always start at the top.

| Strategy | When to Use | Risk |
|----------|------------|------|
| **1. Education & Communication** | Level 1 resistance; information gap is the root cause | Low risk; time investment only |
| **2. Participation & Involvement** | Level 2 resistance; stakeholder has legitimate expertise to contribute | Low risk; may slow decisions but increases buy-in |
| **3. Facilitation & Support** | Level 2 resistance; emotional barriers require structured support (training, coaching, transition assistance) | Low risk; resource investment |
| **4. Negotiation** | Stakeholder has real power and real losses; trade-offs are possible | Medium risk; sets precedent for future negotiations |
| **5. Manipulation / Co-optation** | Need to overcome resistance quickly; giving a role or voice to gain compliance | High risk — if perceived as tokenism, destroys trust permanently |
| **6. Coercion** | Last resort; all other strategies failed; timeline forces action | Very high risk — damages trust long-term; may trigger Level 3 resistance in others |

### Legitimate vs. Emotional Resistance

Before selecting an intervention, differentiate:

| Dimension | Legitimate Pushback | Emotional Resistance |
|-----------|-------------------|---------------------|
| **Specificity** | Specific, evidence-based concerns | Non-specific, shifts rationale when addressed |
| **Consistency** | Consistent position across conversations | Position varies by audience or mood |
| **Content** | Articulates design flaws, identifies real risks | Correlates with personal loss or status change |
| **Response to information** | Adjusts position when new evidence is presented | Maintains position regardless of evidence |
| **Value** | Often contains the most valuable input — listen first | Requires empathy, not data |

**Critical rule:** Always listen first. Some of the most valuable feedback comes
from resisters. Dismissing all resistance as "negativity" is a junior-level
anti-pattern.

## 4. Coalition Building for Hostile High-Power Stakeholders

When a high-power stakeholder is actively resistant (Manage Closely quadrant +
Level 2 or Level 3 resistance), standard engagement strategies may be insufficient.

### Approach: Indirect Influence via Coalition

1. **Map the influence network.** Identify who the hostile stakeholder listens to,
   respects, or defers to. These are your coalition targets.
2. **Build commitment in the coalition first.** Move coalition members to Committed
   or Advocating stage before engaging the hostile stakeholder directly.
3. **Create social proof.** When the hostile stakeholder sees respected peers
   supporting the initiative, Level 3 ("I Don't Trust You") resistance is
   addressed indirectly through trusted intermediaries.
4. **Never bypass.** The goal is not to go around the stakeholder. The goal is to
   create an environment where support is the social norm and resistance requires
   justification.
5. **Offer face-saving.** Provide an on-ramp that allows the stakeholder to shift
   position without appearing to capitulate: new information, changed scope, or
   a design adjustment that addresses their stated concern.

### Escalation Decision

| Scenario | Action |
|----------|--------|
| Hostile + High Power + Level 1 | Direct engagement with education; coalition not needed |
| Hostile + High Power + Level 2 | Direct involvement + coalition building in parallel |
| Hostile + High Power + Level 3 | Coalition-first strategy; direct engagement via trusted intermediaries |
| Hostile + High Power + Sabotage | Escalation to sponsor/leadership; HR involvement if warranted |

## 5. Standard Audience Profile Template

Use this template for each key stakeholder or stakeholder group in the project's
stakeholder register.

```
### [Name / Role / Group]

- **Power:** High / Medium / Low
- **Interest:** High / Medium / Low
- **Grid Quadrant:** Manage Closely / Keep Satisfied / Keep Informed / Monitor
- **Current Commitment Stage:** Unaware / Aware / Understanding / Committed / Advocating
- **Target Commitment Stage:** [stage] — [rationale for target]
- **Resistance Level (if applicable):** None / Level 1 / Level 2 / Level 3
- **Communication Frequency:** Daily / Weekly / Biweekly / Monthly / Per-milestone
- **Preferred Channel:** Email / Teams / In-person / Formal report
- **Methodology Context:** Agile / Waterfall / SAFe / Neutral
- **Key Concerns:** [What keeps this stakeholder up at night regarding this initiative]
- **Engagement Strategy:** [Specific approach — not generic "keep informed"]
- **Influence Network:** [Who does this stakeholder listen to; who do they influence]
- **Regression Risk:** [What could cause commitment to regress; early warning signs]
```

### Profile Refresh Cadence

| Event | Action |
|-------|--------|
| Monthly (standard) | Review all profiles for position changes; update commitment stages |
| Milestone completion | Re-assess interest and power for all Manage Closely and Keep Satisfied stakeholders |
| Scope change | Re-assess all profiles — scope changes shift interest and power unpredictably |
| Reorg / leadership change | Full profile rebuild for affected stakeholders and their influence networks |
| Go-live | Shift profiles to hypercare context — end user profiles become highest priority |

### 5-Second-Scan Bar (per-audience calibration)

Meeting materials (agenda / recap / invite / context block) are built for a 5-second scan per the
canonical specs ([`meeting-agenda-format.md`](../../../../core/standards/meeting-agenda-format.md) /
[`meeting-recap-format.md`](../../../../core/standards/meeting-recap-format.md) § 5-Second-Scan Design).
The scan principles are constant — decision/ask-first, inline 1–3 sentence summaries (no bare links),
owner-tagged + time-boxed items, ask above the fold — but the **bar** calibrates to the audience class.
Apply the matching row when running the meeting-type scan test:

| Audience class | Scan bar | What surfaces above the fold | Detail tolerance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Exec** | Tightest. Decision/ask in the **first line**; ≤3 agenda items visible; **no backstory above the fold**. | The decision + the ask + the deadline. | Context compressed to one line each; detail moves to an appendix/link *with* an inline summary. |
| **Team** | Ask-first, but more operational detail tolerated **below the fold**. | The ask + the owned actions. | Operational specifics (owners, sequencing, sub-items) acceptable below the fold. |
| **Technical** | Ask-first; inline summaries may carry **one technical specific**; links permitted **with** the inline summary (never bare). | The decision/ask + the technical crux. | Technical detail welcome, but each source link still carries its 1–3 sentence inline summary. |

The inline-summary hard rule (no bare links) holds for **every** audience class — the calibration scales
how much detail sits below the fold and how tight the above-the-fold lead is, never whether a summary is
required.

## 6. Change Champion Network Design

Proportional to change scope, the champion network provides distributed
communication, support, and feedback channels.

| Tier | Role | Ratio | Selection Criteria | Responsibilities |
|------|------|-------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Department Leads** | Governance interface | 1 per department | Management authority; decision rights for their area | Cascade communications; escalate department-specific concerns; validate readiness |
| **Power Users** | Training + first-line support | 1:25-50 impacted users (standard); 1:8 (go-live intensive) | Domain expertise; peer credibility (not title); positive influence | Conduct peer training; answer day-of questions; provide usage feedback |
| **Advocates** | Cultural reinforcement | Organic (identified, not appointed) | Natural enthusiasm; respected by peers; early adopters | Share success stories; normalize new behaviors; provide social proof |

**Selection rule:** Peer credibility, not title, is the primary selection criterion.
A respected individual contributor is a better champion than a disengaged manager.

**Anti-pattern:** Appointing champions by org chart position rather than influence.
Champions selected by title without peer credibility become another management
communication channel — they do not shift culture.
